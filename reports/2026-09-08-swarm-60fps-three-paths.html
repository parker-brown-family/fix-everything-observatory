Three paths to sixty frames

The swarm is not slow because the motes are big, or because they glow, or because they pile up. It is slow because canvas 2D charges about four times as much per draw call once a frame crosses roughly 1,750 operations — and the frame issues around 3,600. Every path below is a way under that ceiling. The cheapest one is not the rewrite I proposed on the ticket.

The three paths

Path A · one draw call

Move the motes to WebGL

<0.1ms for 3,529 motes

One instanced draw call replaces 3,529. The per-frame budget stops existing rather than being managed. Measured in a standalone bench whose canvas-2D control reproduced the real app to within 4%.

Verdict: the permanent answer, and the only one that still works at ten times the population. Roughly a session of work.

Path B · stay under the ceiling

Draw fewer than 1,700 things

1.7ms for 1,400 motes

Draw the motes that carry signal and represent the rest as density or a sample. Cost falls off a cliff in your favour: the same sprites cost 1.1µs each below the ceiling and 4.7µs above it.

Verdict: cheapest route to 60fps and no new technology — but the instrument stops showing every artifact as itself.

Path C · draw it less often

Cache the swarm as a layer

~0ms to composite

Render the swarm to an offscreen canvas, composite it every frame, rebuild it a few times a second. Compositing a full 1276×780 layer is below the clock's resolution.

Verdict: smallest diff of the three, and it buys the least — the swarm's motion becomes stepped, including during replay.

Read first

  1. The scope I wrote on the ticket yesterday was wrong. It said drawImage costs 15.2ms because of fill and overdraw, and that canvas 2D therefore could not reach 60fps. The mechanism is wrong: cost is per call, not per pixel, and it is a cliff rather than a slope. The conclusion — that WebGL is the best answer — happens to survive, but not for the reason given, and Path B only exists because the mechanism turned out to be different. contradicted
  2. The ceiling is per frame, not per mote. Adding 400 unrelated draw operations before the motes moved the cliff down by exactly 400. It is a budget for the whole frame — the background, rings, hive, lane and flow diagram all spend from it. measured
  3. Sprite size and overdraw are both irrelevant. Replacing every mote with an 8px dot: 11.9ms versus 11.1ms. Same motes at 18.5× overdraw and at 2.7×: 11.2ms versus 11.4ms. This kills "shrink the glow" and "spread the swarm out" before either becomes a plan. measured
  4. Even with the motes free, 7.4ms of every frame is JavaScript. With every canvas drawing call stubbed out, the frame still costs 7.4ms — the event lane walking ~40k stream rows, the feed, the DOM. It fits inside a 16.7ms budget, but it is the next wall and it caps all three paths at roughly 130fps. measured
  5. One of my own measurements lied, and it is worth knowing which. "Spreading the swarm 1.5× drops the blit from 12ms to 3.4ms" was real and meaningless: the extra motes had moved off the edge of the canvas and were being clipped, not drawn cheaply. Numbers in this brief were re-taken after that was caught. contradicted

What the measurements settle

WebGL does not merely win, it leaves the scale of the problem.

3,529 motes with glow: below the noise floor. 105,870: 0.166ms. 352,900 — a hundred times today's swarm: 0.83ms. The same bench's canvas-2D control took 10.8ms for 3,529, reproducing the real app, so the comparison is like for like. measured

Path B's saving is super-linear, which is why it is viable at all.

Cutting the swarm from 3,529 to 1,400 does not save 60% of 11ms — it saves 85%, because the survivors also stop paying the penalty. That is what makes "draw less" a real answer here rather than a shrug. measured

Path C helps least where smoothness matters most.

Compositing a cached layer is free, but the cache is invalidated by the two things that move: the replay cursor and the orbital motion. It works by quantising both to about 15 updates a second. During playback — the moment the instrument is most on show — that is exactly where the eye is. inferred

The narrow-window case is worse than the number on the ticket.

In a 560px-wide panel the swarm crushes into a 272×141 box at 18.5× overdraw and runs at 40fps. It is the same 11ms of blits — the cost does not care — but it is the layout where the picture is least readable and the frame rate lowest at once. measured

Yours to decide

Which path?

All three reach 60fps on the measurements. They differ in what they spend: A spends build time, B spends the picture, C spends motion.

Recommendation Path A. It is the only one that does not trade something the instrument exists to do, and it is the only one that survives the observatory watching several repositories at once. B and C both manage a ceiling; A removes it.

Is "you cannot see every mote anyway" true enough to build on?

It is the argument Path B rests on. At 18.5× overdraw in a narrow panel it is plainly true. On a wide screen the overdraw is 2.7× and the individuals really are visible, so the argument weakens exactly where most people will look.

Recommendation Do not build B on it. If B is chosen, choose it for the build cost, not because the motes are claimed to be invisible.

Do we take the stopgap while the rewrite waits?

C is a few hours and gets a smooth-enough instrument today; A is a session and gets the real thing. Doing C first means writing code that A deletes.

Recommendation No. The gap between them is one session, and the throwaway costs more in review than the wait costs in frames.

Is the 7.4ms of per-frame JavaScript worth its own ticket now?

It caps every path at about 130fps and it grows with the stream, which grows with every repository inducted. It is not urgent at one project; it might be at six.

Recommendation File it, do not fix it. It needs its own measurement pass, and the event lane walking 40k rows a frame is the obvious first suspect.

How this was measured

and the four instruments that lied first

The method

The animation loop is driven by hand — requestAnimationFrame is throttled in a background tab, so timing it there measures the throttle. Each figure is the median of 25 to 40 hand-driven frames. Costs are separated by stubbing one canvas method at a time (drawImage, fill, stroke, fillRect, fillText) and taking differences.

The clock cannot see a WebGL frame

performance.now() is clamped here — timing an empty statement returns 0, so anything under about a millisecond is invisible. The WebGL figures were taken by forcing each frame to rasterise with a one-pixel readPixels, measuring a no-draw floor (0.366ms) and subtracting it. The GPU timer-query extension is present but never returns a result in this browser, so it could not be used.

Frames nobody presents are frames nobody renders

Before the readback was added, the bench reported 352,900 motes in 0.01ms. That was not a fast GPU; it was the driver discarding work for frames that were never composited. A benchmark that never presents is measuring command submission.

The spread that was really a crop

Scaling mote positions outward appeared to cut the blit from 12ms to 3.4ms, suggesting overdraw was the cause and "spread the swarm" was the fix. The motes had simply moved off the edge of a 236px-wide canvas and were being clipped. Re-measured on a forced desktop layout where every mote lands on canvas, spreading changes nothing.

The window was lying about the layout

The browser tab runs beside a side panel, so the page had a 560px viewport and the swarm a 236px canvas — not the layout anyone uses. The desktop figures come from forcing the page's grid to 1276×780 and re-running sizeCanvas(). Both layouts are reported here rather than averaged, because they behave differently.

What would prove the cliff wrong

If the step is an artifact of this machine's compositor, it will not appear on another GPU or on a software-rendered canvas. Re-run the sweep on an integrated GPU and with hardware acceleration disabled before building Path B on it — B is the only path whose whole design rests on where the cliff sits. A and C do not care.

Path A in detail

one instanced draw call, and what it costs to build

Why the instance buffer is static

Mote position is a pure function of per-artifact constants seeded from the issue number (angle, direction, phase, band), the artifact's own timestamps, and three frame-varying scalars: the cursor, the motion clock, and the zone. So the position belongs in the vertex shader, and the per-instance buffer is uploaded once when a manifest loads and never touched again. Per frame the CPU sets three uniforms and issues one call — no per-mote work at all, which also deletes the 0.6ms currently spent in posOf.

What the shaders do

Vertex: expand a unit quad to radius + glow, place it, and collapse the instance to zero size when the artifact is not alive at the cursor — so culling happens on the GPU and the CPU stops filtering. Fragment: a signed-distance circle or diamond, a radial falloff standing in for shadowBlur, a ring instead of a fill when the artifact was never scored, and the red reopened ring.

What it costs

A second canvas beneath the existing one, both sized by sizeCanvas(); the lane, flow diagram, rings, hive, sparks, hover and selection stay exactly where they are on the 2D layer above. Roughly 300 lines including the GLSL. Hit-testing moves off the frame loop into the pointer handler, which is strictly less work than today. A 2D fallback stays for machines without WebGL2.

What it changes on screen

The glow will not be pixel-identical — shadowBlur is a gaussian and a shader falloff is not — and sub-pixel positions stop being rounded to integers, so the swarm will look slightly smoother. Both need an eye on a before-and-after, because colour is how attribution is read. The hollow never-scored mote must survive the port; a filled dim dot would say the artifact was scored and found clean.

Path B in detail

what a 1,500-mote frame would actually draw

The budget

Roughly 1,700 canvas operations per frame, shared with everything else that draws. The background, rings, hive, event lane and flow diagram already spend a few dozen to a few hundred of those, so the mote allowance is about 1,400 to be safe.

What gets an individual mote

Everything that carries signal: today that is 307 agent-smelling and 133 hint artifacts, plus anything reopened, hovered or selected. That is around 500 — well inside budget, and they are the motes a reader is actually looking for.

What happens to the other 3,000

They still have to appear, or the instrument starts lying about population. The honest options are a density field — one accumulation pass, then a single image — or a fixed sample drawn as individuals with the count stated. A density field keeps the total truthful; a sample keeps the texture of individuals. Either is a visual-language change and wants a mockup before it is built.

The risk that matters

The instrument's argument is that every artifact is a mote and nothing is aggregated away. Path B softens that claim, and the coverage chips would have to say so in the same voice they use for the fetch horizon.

Path C in detail

cache the layer, and quantise what moves

The mechanism

Draw the swarm into an offscreen canvas; each frame, composite that one image and draw only the live overlays — hover ring, selection, sparks — on top. Compositing a full 1276×780 layer measured below the clock's resolution, so the per-frame cost of the swarm effectively disappears.

Why it is not free

Two things invalidate the cache every frame: the replay cursor changes which artifacts are alive and how old they are, and the motion clock rotates the whole field. Caching therefore means quantising both — rebuilding the layer perhaps 15 times a second and letting the swarm step between rebuilds while the transport, feed and flow diagram stay at 60.

Where it shows

Parked on the live view, stepping at 15fps under a slow default motion of 0.5× would be hard to notice. During playback the whole field is in motion and the cursor is sweeping, which is precisely when a step becomes visible — and playback is the instrument's set piece.

What it is genuinely good for

It is the cheapest thing on this page and it is nearly all reversible. If 60fps were needed this week for a demo, this is the one to reach for.
